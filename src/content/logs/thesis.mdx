<h2 id="overview">Overview</h2>
Continuum robots (often abbreviated as CRs) are these awesome bio-inspired devices that I had the 
pleasure of working with for my undergraduate thesis project. Much like an octopus' tentacle or an anteater's tongue,
these robots specialize and offer distinct advantages in navigating confined spaces due to their slender shape and 
physical compliance. For this reason, they have been researched for many years for use in applications such as minimally 
invasive surgery.

Unfortunately, the compliance of these devices is also a curse in addition to a blessing, as they can often sag,
buckle, and vibrate undesireably as a result of interactions with their environment. My thesis project centered 
around minimizing some of these effects with new control strategies for tendon-driven continuum robots (TDCRs), 
a popular variant of CRs driven by internal cables called tendons.


<h3>Magnetic TDCRs and the "Snapping" Problem</h3>
The particular situation my project focused on is what you see in the video below. Folks at the 
*<a href="https://crl.utm.utoronto.ca/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="text-blue-600 hover:underline">Continuum Robotics Laboratory</a>*
have been looking into joining multiple CRs as an approach for increasing stiffeness for force application tasks. 
This is potentially a great solution, as each robot would be navigated into the operating space with it's own 
compliance and manipulability, and only join to brace each other for some high-load task near the region of interest.

My thesis mentor Ella was working on a version of this idea, creating TDCRs which can join to each other using 
passive embedded magnets, when she noticed that unjoining her protoypes often resulted these erratic "snaps". 
Even with design changes such as making one of the robots retractable, it was hard to avoid this situation where 
elastic energy would build up in the robots trying to unjoin until the magnets finally gave way, flinging them 
every which way.

<h2>The Approach</h2>
To solve this instability, I proposed a solution based on applying a control scheme to perform active tendon compensation driven by 
high-speed inertial feedback. In other words, my plan was to sense the acceleration spikes during magnetic unjoining, 
and dynamically adjust the tension in the robot's tendons to actively dampen and counteract the elastic release.
This idea to use the existing means of actuation of the robot to counteract snapping behavior keeps the robot simple, and saves 
the added size and physical complexity (in an already size constrained medical device) that would come with an add-on 
type solution. The trade-off is of course added complexity in the control (and a source of feedback) needed to acheive damping 
through dynamic real-time tension adjustments.

<h3>Simplifying the Problem</h3>
Given that this is a complex problem with many facets and a limited amount of time to address, my supervisors and mentor strongly 
encouraged me to scope down and simplify as much as possible, and I'm glad they did. I ended up deciding on working with a 
simplified version of this problem depicted below, where instead of looking at the full case of two magnetic TDCRs unjoining in 
3D with it's many variations, I would create a test setup where I could look at the snapping response of a single TDCR constrained 
to a 2D plane joining distally with a fixed set of magnets. This way, I could study the simplest form of the snapping problem in the 
hopes of devloping control methods that could be extended in the future.

<h2>Building the Test Setup</h2>
To actually conduct experiments described above, I needed to integrate the 2D magnetic CR and fixed magnets in a system which
could control the robot, observe it's motion to gauge performance, and collect/act on inertial measurments. I already had at my 
disposal the *Dynamixel* smart servos and the driver board that the lab usually uses to drive CRs, and worked on top of this 
to create a useful test setup. The typical workflow was to have a central PC running a python program and sending commands to the motor 
controller over USB through the use of the *Dynamixel SDK*. To allow for the clean seperation of subsystems, as well as parallelism 
which would allow me to run my control loops as fast as possible, I decided to add an additional 2 USB branches which would be 
responsible for relaying the intertial feedback and readings of the robot's motion over time. As you'll see, the final software
achitecture will have dedicated parallel Python processes parsing these incoming data streams and dumping them into shared memory
for a main process handling the robot's control to use as it sees fit.

<h3>Small Side Quest: Camera Tracking</h3>
For the purposes of gathering experimental data on snapping response during experiments, I decided on a simple yet effective
solution. Snapping is most pronounced (and arguably most important, being the location of the tool/payload) at the tip of the robot, 
and through adding a small colored marker to the tip, an overhead camera can easily capture the tip positon over time. This can 
be used to get useful metrics such as snapping amplitude, frequency, or duration. It's as easy as extracting a color mask in OpenCV and 
computing it's centroid frame by frame (+ some de-noising and mm conversion steps but you get the idea), something which can be done 
quickly and reliably.

<h3>Big Side Quest: Compact IMU PCB</h3>
Unlike the camera tracking which probably took a total of 30 minutes to get working, the IMU integration for this project turned into
one of the more involved and satisfying parts of the whole thing. It was my first time making a PCB this tiny, I had a ton of fun, 
and I learned a whole bunch about IMUs in the process.

At first I thought that I could just just slap an off-the-shelf breakout board on my robot, but I quickly realized that:
<ul>
  <li>
  At 10mm in diameter, my robot didn't have the real estate to accommodate typical breakout boards (around 20-30mm) without 
  significantly changing the dynamics of the robot (i.e., a big fat sensor board swinging around on the front of a tiny robot
  would ruin everything).
  </li>
  <li>
  IMU ICs are not created equal and the widely available breakout boards often feature only a small subset of these ICs, with 
  features/layouts favoring convenience and versatility over compactness and performance.
  </li>
</ul>

And so I needed a custom board to fit my needs. After some experiments to determine the vibration frequencies I could expect from my
robot and some research on IMUs and the specs of different IC offerings, I settled on a chip to build my board around. The LSM6DSV16X 
by STMicroelectronics is the sensor I settled on, a high performance 6-axis IMU featuring a tiny 2.5x3mm footprint, good range and 
low noise, an ODR up to 7.68Khz, and some existing firmware libraries so I wouldn't have to start from zero.

My PCB design was able to squeeze in everything I needed to poll the sensor over SPI (or I2C by completing some solder bridges) in a 
10mm diameter 2 layer board, fitting snugly within the spacer disks of my robot. Small contact pads on the back of the board 
allow neat routing of power and communication wires down and away from the robot tip. In a real application, these wires would be 
routed down an internal channel within the robot, but for convenience and debugging, I let them flow outside the robot for my test 
setup. Soldering the teeny tiny enameled wires onto the back of this board was super annoying, but seeing it work for the first time made 
me forget all of that. So cool.

<h3>Baseline</h3>
Of course, the first step is always to get a baseline to compare future results against. Commanding a distal join in the test setup and 
naively trying to return to the neutral position results in a response shown by the IMU and camera tacking data below. As expected,
we see an elastic release causing the robot tip to swing back and forth, resulting in an overshoot of about 25 mm.

<h3>What Worked: IMU Triggered Compensation Profiles</h3>
After some time trying and failing, what ended working was a simple approach of tuning a pre-programmed compensation profile to be 
executed when the IMU detected a snap. These profiles consisted of a series of position setpoints sent in 50ms intervals for the 
robot's motors to follow, designed to counteract the elastic release and dampen the tip's motion to the neutral position. The IMU's 
high-speed feedback allowed for quick detection of the snap event via an acceleration and/or jerk threshold crossing, triggering 
the compensation profile below, shown in terms of tendon lengths corresponding to the motor position setpoints.

<figure class="my-8 flex flex-col items-center">
  <Image src={comp_profile} alt="Compensation Profile Plot" class="max-w-3xl w-full rounded-lg shadow-sm border border-slate-200" />
</figure>

With the compensation profile executed during snapping, the results were quite promising. The overshoot was reduced from 25 mm down 
to under 3 mm, showing that active tendon compensation can minimize snapping behavior. In addition, this approach could be generalized 
to a more general 3D snapping case, taking the intial direction and magnitude of the sudden acceleration into account to dynamically 
generate a compensation profile to execute. The side-by-side videos below show the substantial difference between the baseline regular unjoining 
and the IMU-triggered compensation profile. Seeing the robot stop dead like that for the first time had me doubting my sanity for a second, 
but after trying it 10 more times and confirming it really did work, it was a great feeling for sure.

<h3>Full Feedback Control: Not Quite</h3>
While the compensation profile approach worked great on my test setup, relying on pre-programmed profiles is not easily generalizable across all 
unjoining scenarios, and especially across different TDCRs. To address this lack of universality, full active feedback control was my next goal for
more effective solution.

<figure class="my-8 flex flex-col items-center">
  <Image src={control_law} alt="Control Law Equation" class="max-w-2xl w-full rounded-lg shadow-sm border border-slate-200" />
</figure>

Using a piecewise constant curvature (PCC) kinematic model for my robot which represents each segment in my robot as a constant curvature arc, 
I came up with a simple control law to try. This law aims to both drive the robot to a desired pose (paramaterized by its two segment curvatures) while
also damping large lateral tip accelerations using the IMU's accelerometer. The segment weighting on the IMU term above stems from the fact that this 
one-dimensional IMU measurement needs to translate into a two-dimensional control action. Intuitively, we know that the curvature of the base segment 
influences the tip position far more than the tip segment due to a lever arm effect. To determine the appropriate weighting, I used the PCC model 
description of the lateral tip coordinate $X(\kappa_1, \kappa_2)$ to compute the Jacobian of the tip position with respect to the segment curvatures.
If you work through the math, you get something like this:

$$
X(\kappa_1, \kappa_2) = \frac{1 - \cos(\kappa_1 L_1)}{\kappa_1} + \frac{\cos(\kappa_1 L_1) - \cos(\kappa_1 L_1 + \kappa_2 L_2)}{\kappa_2}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial X}{\partial \kappa_1} = \frac{\kappa_1 L_1 \sin(\kappa_1 L_1) + \cos(\kappa_1 L_1) - 1}{\kappa_1^2} + \frac{L_1 (\sin(\kappa_1 L_1 + \kappa_2 L_2) - \sin(\kappa_1 L_1))}{\kappa_2}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial X}{\partial \kappa_2} = \frac{\kappa_2 L_2 \sin(\kappa_1 L_1 + \kappa_2 L_2) + \cos(\kappa_1 L_1 + \kappa_2 L_2) - \cos(\kappa_1 L_1)}{\kappa_2^2}
$$


Computing the ratio of the two partials $\frac{\partial X}{\partial \kappa_1} : \frac{\partial X}{\partial \kappa_2}$ for different configurations around 
the robot's neutral position (which was my desired end pose for my tests), I found that this ratio sticks pretty closely to 3:1, yielding the normalized 
weighting of $\begin{bmatrix} 0.25 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}^T$ found in the control law.

The results? Not quite what I was looking for. With the controller active and the tendons pre-tensioned for maximum control sensitivity, the robot's 
initial snapping overshoot was somewhat reduced from a 14 mm baseline down to 8 mm. However, the settling time actually increased from the uncontrolled 
baseline of 309 ms to 404 ms. While my control loop running on the PC was spitting out commands at over 200 Hz, it seems the physical action frequency 
of the system was bottlenecked. As a result, the physical tendon response lagged behind the robot's movement, inadvertently pumping energy into the 
vibration instead of damping it out.

<h2>Take-Aways & What's Next</h2>
Overall, I had a great time with this project. It was a unique opportunity to really start from a difficult problem and propose and develop a mechatronic 
solution from the ground up. It successfully proved that tip-mounted IMUs and active tendon compensation can be effective for stabilizing magnetically 
joinable continuum robots in certain scenarios, keeping them within surgical safety margins. However, I'm not ready to give up just yet. The mechanical 
lag from the smart servos which led to feedback control being ineffective is a problem I think can be solved with more agile actuators and a more 
optimized system architecture, and so I'm continuing my work into the summer with the goal of publishing a paper to a conference like ICRA. 
For this next iteration, I plan to move away from the PC-based Python and USB stack. Instead, I'll be porting the control over to run entirely in bare 
metal C on a high clock speed microcontroller to achieve the fastest control loops possible. For the actuators, I will be upgrading to direct-drive brushless motors. By 
utilizing high-performance controllers communicating over a high-speed CAN-FD bus for torque (rather than position) control, I hope to achieve a much 
more agile system capable of real-time snapping compensation. If you're interested in that, stay tuned for a future post where I will be detailing my progress!
